    title: "What Is the Deadlift?"
    body: |
      The deadlift is exactly what it sounds like: you pick a heavy thing up off the ground. That's it.

      The deadlift is the most natural of all barbell lifts. Every time you pick up a suitcase, a bag of groceries, or a toddler, you're deadlifting. The barbell version just lets you add weight.

      **Why it matters:**
      - Builds strength from head to toe
      - Strengthens your back, hips, and grip
      - Teaches you how to pick things up safely for the rest of your life
      - Is the foundation for the clean and the snatch

      Most people are afraid of the deadlift because they think it hurts your back. The opposite is true. Done correctly, the deadlift protects your back. Done incorrectly, anything hurts your back.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Deadlift"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Stance: hip width to shoulder width
      - Bar: over mid-foot, in contact with shins
      - Hands: just outside knees, symmetrical grip (parallel, hook, or mixed)
      - Shoulders: slightly in front of the bar
      - Chest up, back arched, arms locked straight

      **Execution:**
      - Take a breath, brace
      - Drive through heels
      - Hips and shoulders rise at the same rate
      - Once bar passes knees, hips open all the way
      - Bar stays in contact with legs the whole time
      - Lock out: stand tall, squeeze glutes, don't hyperextend
      - Lower: push hips back first, then bend knees

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Lumbar curve maintained
      - Weight on heels
      - Shoulders slightly in front of bar on setup
      - Hips and shoulders rise at same rate
      - Bar stays in contact with legs
      - At the top, hip completely open and knees straight

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Loss of lumbar curve (rounded back) | Cue "Pull hips back and lift chest"; reduce load until arch can be maintained |
      | Weight shifting to toes | Settle into heels, pull hips back, maintain hamstring tension |
      | Shoulders behind bar on setup | Raise hips to move shoulders over or slightly in front of bar |
      | Hips rise before chest (stiff-legged deadlift) | Cue "Lift chest and hips at same rate" |
      | Bar travels around knees instead of straight up | Cue "Push knees back as chest rises" |
      | Bar collides with knees on descent | Push hips back first, delay knee bend |
      | Bar loses contact with legs | Cue "Pull the bar in" |

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming the Deadlift"
    body: |
      **Loading:**
      - Deadlift loads are usually heavier than squats for most lifters.
      - Bodyweight deadlift = beginner standard
      - 2× bodyweight = good
      - 3× bodyweight = strong
      - Frequency: once per week at near-max loads, maybe one other session at lighter loads.

      **Programming notes:**
      - The deadlift is the most systemically fatiguing lift. High volume deadlift work is rarely productive.
      - Lower volume, higher intensity is standard.
      - Deadlift after squat in the same session is common but requires careful fatigue management.
      - Deadlift variants (block pulls, deficit deadlifts, Romanian deadlifts, trap bar deadlifts) address specific weaknesses.

      **Grip:**
      - Hook grip (both hands overhand, thumbs wrapped inside) — strongest, most painful, requires adaptation.
      - Mixed grip (one over, one under) — strong but can cause bicep tears if arm bends.
      - Parallel grip (both overhand) — limits load but safest and most symmetrical.
      - Straps — allow training grip-independent pulling.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Biomechanics"
    body: |
      **Mechanics:**
      - Deadlift is a hip-hinge pattern with significant knee involvement in the setup and initial pull.
      - Bar path should be vertical, close to the body. Any horizontal distance between bar and body increases the moment arm and thus the load on the spine.
      - The setup position varies with anthropometry: longer femurs and shorter torsos require a more horizontal starting position.

      **Spinal loading:**
      - Lumbar shear forces peak during the initial pull.
      - Proper bracing and neutral spine minimize injury risk.
      - Grip strength is often the limiting factor before lower back strength in advanced lifters.

      **Variants:**
      - **Conventional deadlift:** Narrow stance, more hip-dominant.
      - **Sumo deadlift:** Wide stance, toes out, more quad-dominant, shorter range of motion.
      - **Trap bar deadlift:** Neutral grip, more upright torso, easier on lower back.
      - **Romanian deadlift (RDL):** No floor contact, emphasizes hamstring stretch.
      - **Block pull / rack pull:** Partial range, overloads the top end.
      - **Deficit deadlift:** Elevated floor, increases range of motion.

      **Programming science:**
      - Deadlift responds well to low-frequency, high-intensity programming.
      - 1–2× per week is often optimal for intermediate and advanced lifters.
      - Deadlift is one of the best exercises for total-body strength and neural adaptation.
