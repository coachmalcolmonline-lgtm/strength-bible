    title: "What Is Sled Work?"
    body: |
      Sled work means pushing or pulling a weighted sled across the ground. It's low-impact, high-effort conditioning, and it builds leg drive and mental toughness.

      **Two main variations:**

      - **Sled push:** hands on the uprights, drive the sled forward with your legs
      - **Sled pull:** harness or rope attached; walk backward or forward pulling the sled

      **Why athletes do it:**

      - Zero eccentric loading -- you can train hard without muscle soreness
      - Trains leg drive and hip extension under heavy load
      - Builds conditioning without beating up your joints like running
      - Mental toughness -- sled work is uncomfortable by design

      **What it feels like:** explosive on the first few steps. Then your legs fill with blood, your lungs burn, and you have to fight to keep moving. That's the point.

      Sled work is used everywhere -- football, rugby, track, crossfit, tactical training -- because it works without leaving you wrecked.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform Sled Work"
    body: |
      **Sled Push:**

      - Grip the uprights (or low handles if the sled has them)
      - Arms extended, slight forward lean
      - Drive with alternating legs, like a sprint
      - Full triple extension each step
      - Keep hips low and drive through

      **Sled Pull (harness):**

      - Harness around waist or shoulders, strap to sled
      - Walk forward or backward against resistance
      - Maintain posture -- don't let the sled pull you over
      - Drive with legs, not arms

      **Sled Pull (rope):**

      - Standing hand-over-hand pull, or seated rope pull
      - Keep hips stable, pull with the back and arms
      - Reset position between pulls

      **Primary Points of Performance:**

      - Push: low hips, strong forward lean, powerful steps
      - Pull: harness tight, hips stable, upright or slight lean
      - Breathe steady -- don't sprint into a wall and gas out
      - Reset position if posture breaks down

      **Common Faults:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Standing too upright (push) | Lower hips, lean forward into the sled |
      | Shuffling feet | Shorten stride, drive with full leg extension |
      | Sled drifting sideways | Grip handles evenly; check the track |
      | Shoulders rounding (pull) | Keep chest up, shoulder blades back |
      | Gassing out early | Slow down, breathe, reset |

      **Prescriptions:**

      - Beginner: 4-6 sets of 20m push, light load, 90 sec rest
      - Intermediate: 6-8 sets of 20-30m, moderate load, 90-120 sec rest
      - Advanced: 8-10 sets of 30m, heavy load, or time-based sets (30-60 sec)
      - Conditioning: alternate push/pull, 8-12 rounds with short rest

      **Load:** start light enough to move smoothly. Add weight only when form holds.

  - level: 3
    title: "The Biomechanics of Sled Work"
    body: |
      Sled work is a unique training modality because it's a **concentric-only** movement. Unlike running, jumping, or weightlifting, there's no eccentric phase -- you push, the sled moves, you reset. This is why sled work causes almost no muscle soreness.

      **Force and velocity:**

      - Sled pushes load the leg extensors (quads, glutes, hamstrings, calves) through a full triple-extension cycle
      - Horizontal force output is high -- the sled resists forward movement
      - Velocity decreases as load increases -- sled work is a force-velocity variable

      **Load-velocity relationship:**

      | Load | Velocity | Primary adaptation |
      |---|---|---|
      | Light (5-15% BW) | Fast | Sprint mechanics, acceleration |
      | Moderate (15-30% BW) | Moderate | Power-endurance |
      | Heavy (30-50% BW) | Slow | Max strength, mental toughness |

      **Ground reaction forces:**

      - Sled push GRF is dominated by horizontal force (unlike running, where vertical dominates)
      - This is why sled work transfers directly to acceleration in sprinting
      - Resisted sprinting with a sled is one of the most evidence-supported acceleration-training methods

      **Muscle activation:**

      - Quads and glutes: primary drivers
      - Calves: powerful push-off each step
      - Hamstrings: assist hip extension
      - Core: brace and stabilize
      - Upper body: minimal activation in push; moderate in pull

      **Eccentric-free training:**

      Because there's no eccentric phase, sled work:

      - Produces almost no muscle damage (low CK elevation)
      - Can be trained more frequently than running
      - Is ideal for in-season athletes who need conditioning without soreness
      - Doesn't produce the "legs are wrecked for 3 days" effect of sprinting or heavy eccentric work

      **Programming implications:**

      - Train sled pushes 2-4x per week (even in-season)
      - Use moderate loads (20-30% BW) for power-endurance
      - Use heavy loads (40-50% BW) for strength-endurance
      - Use light loads (5-15% BW) for acceleration development
      - Pair with sprint work in speed-focused blocks
      - Pair with strongman training in event-prep blocks

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Sled Work Science"
    body: |
      At the research level, sled work is studied as a both a conditioning modality and a sprint-development tool with specific force-velocity characteristics.

      **The resisted sprint model:**

      Sled pushes and resisted sprints load the horizontal force vector, which is the limiting factor in acceleration (first 10-20m). Vertical-dominant training (back squats) transfers poorly to acceleration; horizontal-dominant training (sled pushes) transfers directly.

      Optimal load for acceleration training:
      - 10-15% bodyweight for speed-focused work
      - 20-30% BW for moderate acceleration
      - 40-50% BW for force-focused work

      Loads above 50% BW shift the movement toward pure strength and degrade sprint mechanics.

      **Post-activation potentiation (PAP):**

      A heavy sled push before a sprint can potentiate subsequent sprint performance:
      - 3-5 heavy pushes (30-50% BW) for 10-15m
      - 4-6 min rest
      - Sprint 20m

      This is used by many sprint coaches on speed-development days.

      **Conditioning applications:**

      Sled work is ideal for high-intensity conditioning with low recovery cost:

      - 8-12 x 30m moderate load with 60-90 sec rest = intense conditioning session
      - Recovery cost: minimal (no eccentric damage)
      - Cardiovascular load: high (heart rate 85-95% max during sets)
      - Metabolic stress: high (glycolytic-dominant)

      **Advanced variations:**

      **1. Backward sled drag:**
      - Walk backward, pulling the sled
      - Emphasizes quads, knees, and mental toughness
      - Used for knee rehab and quad development

      **2. Lateral sled drag:**
      - Sideways stepping, pulling the sled
      - Trains hip abductors and adductors
      - Good for change-of-direction athletes

      **3. Sled sprint (light load):**
      - 5-15% BW load
      - Sprint mechanics must hold
      - Best transfer to acceleration

      **4. Sled push sprint:**
      - Push with hands high or low
      - High hands = forward lean, sprint mechanics
      - Low hands = more like a football drive block

      **5. Alternating push/pull conditioning:**
      - Push 20m, pull 20m, repeat
      - Full-body metabolic stimulus
      - Brutal; used in tactical and team sport settings

      **Testing benchmarks:**

      | Level | Load (push) | Distance | Time |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Beginner | 20% BW | 20m | 15-25 sec |
      | Intermediate | 40% BW | 20m | 20-30 sec |
      | Advanced | 60% BW | 20m | 25-40 sec |

      **Programming takeaways for advanced athletes:**

      - Use sled work year-round; adapt load to phase
      - Off-season: heavy pushes, longer distances
      - In-season: light pushes, short distances, high frequency
      - Pair with sprint work on speed days (PAP)
      - Use sled work as a low-soreness alternative to running in-season
      - Alternate push/pull for full-body conditioning
      - Track time-to-complete over fixed distance as a progress marker

# Sled Work

Push or pull a weighted sled. No eccentric loading means no soreness, high intensity, and a direct transfer to acceleration. The perfect conditioning tool for in-season athletes.
