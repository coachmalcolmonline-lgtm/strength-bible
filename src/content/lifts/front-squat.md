    title: "What Is the Front Squat?"
    body: |
      The front squat is the back squat's cousin. Same pattern — sit down, stand up. But the bar is in front of you, resting on your shoulders.

      Why does that matter?

      Because the bar in front forces you to stay upright. You can't lean forward without dropping the bar. This makes the front squat more demanding on your quads, your core, and your upper back.

      The front squat is the foundation for the clean and the clean & jerk. It's also a brutal core exercise in disguise.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Front Squat"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Stance: shoulder-width apart
      - Bar: resting on the front of the shoulders, on a "shelf" created by the deltoids
      - Elbows high, upper arms parallel to the ground
      - Hands outside shoulders, loose fingertip grip
      - Chest up, back arched, midsection tight

      **Execution:**
      - Take a breath, brace
      - Send butt back and down
      - Knees track over feet
      - Descend below parallel
      - Drive through heels, stand up
      - Keep elbows high the entire time

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Bar racked properly: elbows high, hands just outside shoulders, bar rests on shoulders with a loose fingertip grip
      - Elbows high throughout the movement
      - All other squat points of performance apply

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Bar not in contact with torso / bar drifting forward | Cue "Elbows high and rest bar on fingertips" |
      | Elbows drop and chest comes forward | Cue "Elbows UP UP UP! And big chest"; tactile cue — place arm under athlete's elbows |
      | All air squat faults | Apply air squat fixes |

      **The Rack Position:**
      The front rack is the hardest part for most beginners. Tight wrists, tight shoulders, or tight lats all make it harder. Practice with an empty bar. Use a cross-arm grip if the wrist rack isn't available yet. Practice front rack stretches.

  - level: 3
    title: "Biomechanics & Why the Front Squat Matters"
    body: |
      **Mechanics:**
      - The front-loaded bar shifts the center of mass forward, requiring a more upright torso to maintain balance.
      - This increases the knee moment (more quad work) and reduces the hip moment (less posterior chain involvement than the back squat).
      - The upper back and core must work hard to prevent the torso from collapsing forward.

      **Programming role:**
      - **Olympic lifting foundation:** The front squat is the receiving position for the clean.
      - **Quad development:** More direct quad stimulus than back squat.
      - **Core strength:** The anti-flexion demand is significant.
      - **Transfer:** Excellent for athletes who need upright strength — sprinting, jumping, combat sports.

      **Load relationship:** Most athletes front squat 75–85% of their back squat. A 1:1 ratio suggests back squat technical limitations or exceptional front squat strength.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Considerations"
    body: |
      **Kinetics:**
      - The anterior bar position increases the demand on the thoracic extensors, cervical extensors, and anterior core.
      - Ankle dorsiflexion requirements are higher than in the back squat due to the forward knee travel needed to keep the bar over the mid-foot.
      - Wrist and elbow mobility determine rack position quality; mobility limits often manifest as technique breakdown before strength limits.

      **Programming:**
      - Front squat loading is typically prescribed as a percentage of clean 1RM, not front squat 1RM, in weightlifting programs.
      - Because of the rack position demand, front squat volume is often periodized separately from back squat volume.
      - For athletes who cannot achieve a clean rack position, front squat variants (Zercher, safety bar front squat) can substitute temporarily.

      **Physiological load:** Lower absolute load, higher relative core and upper-back demand. Different fatigue profile from back squat — often easier to recover from but harder on the wrists and thoracic spine.
