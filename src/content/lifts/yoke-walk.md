    title: "What Is a Yoke Walk?"
    body: |
      The yoke walk -- also called the yoke carry or super yoke -- is a strongman event where you walk with a heavy metal frame across your shoulders.

      The yoke is a steel apparatus with two uprights and a crossbar that sits behind your neck. You load it with plates, duck under it, stand it up, and walk.

      **Why athletes do it:**

      - Trains the entire trunk under massive axial load
      - Builds the spine, traps, and legs in a way nothing else does
      - Brutal mental training -- the weight is trying to crush you and you have to walk anyway
      - Direct carryover to any sport that requires carrying or resisting heavy load

      **What it feels like:** heavy as soon as you stand. Each step is a fight. Your core screams, your traps burn, and you have to keep moving or get crushed. Then you rack it and realize how much harder that was than it looked.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Yoke Walk"
    body: |
      **Setup:**

      - Load the yoke with plates at the desired weight
      - Position yourself under the crossbar (feet between the uprights)
      - Duck under, place the crossbar high on your upper traps (not your neck)
      - Hands grip the uprights or crossbar for stability
      - Stand by driving through your legs, not by pulling with your back

      **The walk:**

      - Stand tall -- don't lean forward or back
      - Short, powerful steps -- not long strides
      - Chest up, ribs down, core braced
      - Breathe steadily -- one breath every 2-3 steps
      - Keep your eyes forward, not down

      **The set-down:**

      - Stop when your posture fails or your time is up
      - Sit the yoke back down under control
      - Duck out from under the crossbar

      **Primary Points of Performance:**

      - Bar high on traps, not on neck
      - Feet wide enough to fit between uprights
      - Even step cadence
      - Upright posture throughout
      - Core braced as if expecting a punch

      **Common Faults:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Bar on neck | Reposition higher on the traps |
      | Leaning forward | Slow down, stand tall, shorten strides |
      | Uneven steps | Practice without load first |
      | Core failing early | Reduce load; brace harder |
      | Swaying side to side | Feet wider; engage obliques |

      **Prescriptions:**

      - Beginner: 3-4 sets of 10m, light load, 2 min rest
      - Intermediate: 4-5 sets of 15-20m, moderate load, 2-3 min rest
      - Advanced: 4-6 sets of 20-30m, heavy load, 3 min rest
      - Competition strongman: 20-30m for time with max load

      **Safety note:** yoke walks are the highest axial loading in any training modality. Do not attempt heavy yokes without coaching and proper technique.

  - level: 3
    title: "The Biomechanics of the Yoke Walk"
    body: |
      The yoke walk is the ultimate test of spinal loading, trunk stability, and gait under extreme axial compression. Loads often exceed 2-3x bodyweight.

      **Spinal loading:**

      - Axial compression: 2-3x bodyweight or higher during heavy yoke walks
      - Shear forces: significant, requiring active bracing
      - Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP): critical for spinal protection
      - Spinal erectors: sustained isometric contraction throughout the walk

      **Grip and upper body:**

      - Traps: sustained isometric load-bearing
      - Shoulders: stabilize the crossbar
      - Hands: grip uprights or bar for balance
      - Upper back: holds the chest up against the compression

      **Gait mechanics:**

      - Step length: shorter than unloaded walking (10-20 cm)
      - Cadence: typically faster to minimize time under load
      - Stride width: wider than normal to prevent swaying
      - Ground contact: quick and firm, minimal oscillation

      **The "brace" effect:**

      Effective yoke walking requires a maximal Valsalva-like brace sustained over 10-30 seconds. This is:

      - Different from a squat's brief Valsalva
      - Demanding on the diaphragm
      - Requires practice to coordinate with breathing
      - Why yoke walks are so mentally taxing

      **Programming implications:**

      - Yoke walks are high-CNS, high-structural load -- 1-2x per week max
      - Full recovery between sets (3+ min)
      - Never train yoke walks tired
      - Build up load slowly -- the spine needs months to adapt to extreme loading
      - Combine with lighter trunk work (carries, planks) for accessory
      - Video every session for posture and gait analysis

      **Testing benchmarks:**

      | Level | Load (% BW) | Distance |
      |---|---|---|
      | Beginner | 50-75% | 10m |
      | Intermediate | 100-150% | 15-20m |
      | Advanced | 200-250% | 20-25m |
      | Elite | 300%+ | 20-30m |

      A 180 lb athlete carrying 360 lbs for 20m is advanced. Elite is 540+ lbs.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Yoke Walk Science"
    body: |
      At the research level, yoke walking is studied as a unique combination of axial loading, gait training, and mental toughness with specific injury risks and adaptation patterns.

      **Spinal load modeling:**

      - Axial compression during a max yoke: 2.5-4.0x bodyweight
      - Shear forces: moderate but sustained
      - Disc compression: significant, especially at L4-L5
      - Adaptation: bones and discs remodel over months to handle the load

      **Structural adaptations:**

      - Increased bone mineral density (axial loading is osteogenic)
      - Tendon stiffness in the spinal erectors and traps
      - Vertebral disc adaptation (nucleus pulposus hydration, annulus fibrosis strength)
      - Increased thoracic kyphosis tolerance

      **Neuromuscular demands:**

      - Sustained near-maximal isometric contraction of trunk
      - Gait coordination under load
      - Balance and proprioception at high loads
      - Rate coding in the traps and erectors exceeds 70 Hz in trained athletes

      **Advanced programming:**

      **1. Load-focused (max strength):**
      - 3-4 sets of 10m at near-max load
      - Full recovery (3-4 min)
      - 1x per week

      **2. Distance-focused (endurance):**
      - 4-5 sets of 20-30m at moderate load
      - 2-3 min rest
      - 1-2x per week

      **3. Time-focused (competition prep):**
      - 3-4 sets of max distance in 60 sec
      - 3 min rest
      - Weekly during prep phase

      **4. Combined with carries:**
      - Yoke walk 20m + farmer carry 20m + sled push 20m
      - 3-4 rounds, 3 min rest between rounds
      - Full-body conditioning

      **Injury considerations:**

      Yoke walks are among the highest-injury-risk strongman events. Common injuries:

      - **Lower back strain:** from rounding during setup or set-down
      - **Neck compression:** from bar placement on the neck instead of traps
      - **Knee stress:** from wide, unstable stance
      - **Wrist strain:** from uneven loading or grip failure

      Prevent with:
      - Progressive loading (no big jumps)
      - Proper bar placement
      - Full warm-up including light yoke walks
      - Video feedback
      - Coaching presence for heavy attempts

      **Testing and benchmarking:**

      Elite strongman competition standards:
      - Heavyweight: 350-500 kg for 20m
      - Middleweight: 250-350 kg for 20m
      - Women's heavyweight: 200-300 kg for 20m

      For non-strongman athletes, 150-200% bodyweight for 20m is an excellent target.

      **Programming takeaways for advanced athletes:**

      - Respect the axial load. Progress slowly over months, not weeks.
      - Never train yoke walks to failure.
      - Always warm up with light yokes before heavy attempts.
      - Combine with farmer carries, sled work, and stone work for full-spectrum strongman development.
      - Deload yoke work every 3-4 weeks.
      - Use a belt for loads above 1.5x bodyweight.
      - If your back feels off, stop. Do not push through spinal fatigue.
