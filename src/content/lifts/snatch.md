    title: "What Is the Snatch?"
    body: |
      The snatch is the fastest lift in the gym. You pick a barbell up off the floor, extend your hips and knees explosively, and catch it **overhead in one motion** -- in a wide squat, arms locked out.

      It's the first of the two Olympic lifts (the other is the clean & jerk). Weightlifting has been an Olympic sport since 1896, and the snatch has been part of it since 1928.

      **Why it matters:**

      - Highest power output of any barbell movement
      - Trains full-body explosiveness
      - Demands mobility, coordination, and precision
      - Transfer to sprinting and jumping is elite

      **Why it's hard:**

      - You have to move the bar from floor to overhead in ~1 second
      - The bar path is unforgiving -- drift forward and you miss
      - The overhead position requires good shoulder and thoracic mobility

      Most athletes don't need a full competition snatch. But even hang snatches or power snatches build explosive power that carries over to sport.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Snatch"
    body: |
      The snatch has the same five positions as the clean, but everything is faster and the catch is overhead.

      **Setup:**

      - Feet hip-width, toes slightly out
      - Bar over mid-foot
      - Wide grip -- hands 1.5-2x shoulder-width, arms straight
      - Hips back and down, chest up, back flat
      - Shoulders slightly in front of the bar
      - Hook grip on the bar (thumb wrapped over index finger)

      **First pull (floor to knee):**

      - Push the floor away
      - Keep back angle constant
      - Bar stays close
      - Hips and shoulders rise at the same rate

      **Second pull (knee to hip):**

      - Explosive hip and knee extension
      - Shrug the shoulders
      - Elbows stay straight, bar close to body
      - Bar brushes the hips

      **Turnover (pull under):**

      - Aggressively pull yourself under the bar
      - Turn the elbows up and out
      - Punch the bar overhead
      - Lock out the elbows

      **Catch (overhead squat position):**

      - Land in a wide squat -- feet wider than your jerk stance
      - Bar overhead, in line with your ears (not behind)
      - Elbows locked, wrists neutral
      - Chest upright, back flat
      - Weight in mid-foot

      **Stand to finish:**

      - Stand all the way up, keeping the bar locked out
      - Reset between reps

      **Primary Points of Performance:**

      - Bar path vertical -- no forward drift
      - Full hip extension before turnover
      - Aggressive pull-under
      - Overhead position active and locked
      - Same foot position each rep

      **Common Faults:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Bar drifts forward | Pull bar toward hips at second pull; drill snatch pulls |
      | Elbows soft at the catch | "Punch the ceiling" cue; overhead squat drills |
      | Wide catch but bar behind ears | Bring the bar forward; drill overhead position |
      | No pull-under | Aggressive hips; "get under it" cue |
      | Bar crashes on catch | Catch actively; don't wait for it |

  - level: 3
    title: "The Biomechanics of the Snatch"
    body: |
      The snatch is the highest-power movement in all of sport. Elite lifters generate up to 50 W/kg during the second pull -- more than sprinting, jumping, or any other measured activity.

      **Key biomechanical features:**

      **Bar path:**
      - Elite snatch bar path is nearly vertical, deviating <5 cm from the vertical line
      - The bar typically "loops" slightly outward at the top of the pull, then back in for the catch
      - Forward drift is the most common technical error and costs kilos

      **Vertical displacement:**
      - Bar starts at ~23 cm, ends at ~200+ cm (for elite lifters)
      - Total displacement: ~180 cm in ~1.0-1.2 seconds
      - Average bar velocity: 1.4-1.7 m/s
      - Peak bar velocity: 1.8-2.0 m/s

      **Ground reaction forces:**
      - Peak vertical GRF: 2.5-3.5x bodyweight during the second pull
      - Braking force during the first pull: 1.0-1.5x bodyweight
      - Reaction time from start to second pull: ~0.6-0.8 seconds

      **The catch position:**

      The overhead squat position requires:
      - Deep squat depth (thighs below parallel or full depth)
      - Active shoulder flexion to lock the bar overhead
      - Thoracic extension to keep the bar over the mid-foot
      - Ankle dorsiflexion to sit deep without falling forward

      Athletes who lack mobility in any of these will struggle to catch heavy snatches.

      **Programming implications:**

      - Snatch technique degrades fast under fatigue -- always train fresh
      - 1-3 reps per set, 5-8 sets total, 2-3 min rest
      - Never train to failure -- technical failure comes before muscular failure
      - Accessory work: overhead squats, snatch balances, snatch pulls, tall snatches
      - Video every rep from the side for bar path feedback

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Snatch Science"
    body: |
      At the research level, the snatch is studied as the highest-power human movement and a window into neuromuscular coordination under maximal load and velocity.

      **Neuromuscular demands:**

      - Near-maximal motor unit recruitment throughout the pull
      - Intermuscular coordination is extreme -- over 20 muscles synchronize across three phases
      - Rate coding exceeds 60 Hz in the second pull in trained lifters
      - The lift is bilateral, but the receiving position requires unilateral stability

      **The three-pull model vs. the six-phase model:**

      Two frameworks describe snatch technique:

      **Three-pull model (traditional):**
      1. First pull: floor to knee
      2. Second pull (transition + explosion): knee to hip
      3. Third pull (pull-under): hips to overhead catch

      **Six-phase model (biomechanical):**
      1. Start
      2. First pull
      3. Transition
      4. Second pull
      5. Turnover
      6. Catch and recovery

      The six-phase model is used in modern sports science because it separates the transition (where the athlete re-bends the knees) from the second pull.

      **Hook grip:**

      Most elite lifters use a hook grip -- the thumb wraps around the bar and is trapped by the index and middle fingers. This:
      - Prevents the bar from rolling out of the hands at heavy loads
      - Keeps the wrists neutral (unlike a traditional grip, which forces the wrist into flexion)
      - Takes weeks to adapt -- uncomfortable at first, essential at heavy weights

      **Mobility requirements:**

      To catch a heavy snatch, an athlete needs:
      - Overhead shoulder flexion: 180° with the bar locked out and stable
      - Thoracic extension: enough to keep the chest upright
      - Hip flexion with external rotation: for a wide squat stance
      - Ankle dorsiflexion: 35-45° for full-depth catch

      Athletes with limited mobility can still snatch, but usually with a modified catch (power snatch, or split snatch) or a wider stance. Mobility work should be part of any snatch program.

      **Programming models for advanced athletes:**

      **1. Bulgarian-style (high frequency):**
      - Snatch daily, alternating heavy/light
      - 8-10 sessions per week
      - Very high technical demand, elite only

      **2. Russian-style (block periodization):**
      - 4-6 week blocks of high volume, then high intensity
      - 4-5 sessions per week
      - Matches the traditional Soviet model

      **3. Sports-performance model (for non-Oly athletes):**
      - 2-3 snatch sessions per week
      - Low volume, moderate intensity (70-85%)
      - Emphasis on power transfer, not max load
      - Combines with strength and speed work

      **Testing and benchmarking:**

      | Level | Snatch 1RM (as % BW) |
      |---|---|
      | Beginner | 0.4× BW |
      | Intermediate | 0.6× BW |
      | Advanced | 0.9× BW |
      | Elite | 1.2× BW+ |

      A 180 lb athlete snatching 108 lbs is intermediate. Advanced is 162 lbs. Elite is 216+ lbs.

      **Programming takeaways for advanced athletes:**

      - Technique before load, always. One bad rep erases ten good ones.
      - Video every session. Bar path doesn't lie.
      - Build overhead mobility in parallel with strength -- they gate progress.
      - Snatch is the most technique-sensitive lift -- never train it fatigued.
      - Drop the lift when it stops being technical -- don't grind through misses.
      - Integrate with jerk work -- most athletes lose meet attempts on the jerk, not the snatch.

# Snatch

The snatch is the highest-power human movement -- floor to overhead in one motion. Technical, demanding, and the gold standard for developing explosive athletic power.
