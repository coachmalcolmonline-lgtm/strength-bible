    title: "What Is a Farmer Carry?"
    body: |
      The farmer carry is exactly what it sounds like: pick up something heavy in each hand and walk with it.

      That's it. No barbell, no rack, no platform. Just grip, posture, and steps.

      **Why athletes do it:**

      - Builds grip strength like nothing else
      - Trains the entire trunk to stay braced under load
      - Carries over to every sport -- you're always carrying, holding, or resisting
      - Simple to learn, brutal to do

      **What it looks like in training:** two heavy dumbbells, kettlebells, or dedicated farmer handles. Pick them up, stand tall, walk 20-40 meters, set them down. Rest. Repeat.

      It sounds easy. It isn't. After 40 meters with heavy weight, your forearms, traps, and core will tell you what real work feels like.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Farmer Carry"
    body: |
      **Setup:**

      - Stand between two implements (dumbbells, kettlebells, farmer handles)
      - Feet hip-width, toes slightly out
      - Hinge down, flat back, chest up
      - Grip each handle firmly -- crush grip
      - Stand up by driving through your heels and extending your hips

      **The walk:**

      - Stand tall -- don't lean back or forward
      - Shoulders back and down, chest proud
      - Core braced as if bracing for a punch
      - Walk with controlled, even steps
      - Breathe steadily -- don't hold your breath the whole time

      **The set-down:**

      - Stop walking when you feel grip or posture failing
      - Hinge at the hips, not the back
      - Set the weights down under control
      - Don't drop unless you're in a competition

      **Primary Points of Performance:**

      - Tall posture throughout
      - Even stride length
      - Shrug shoulders slightly up to prevent them from sagging
      - Core tight, ribs down
      - Grip locked, knuckles facing forward

      **Common Faults:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Leaning back | Reduce load; keep ribs down, chest proud |
      | Shoulders sagging | Shrug slightly, keep traps engaged |
      | Short, choppy steps | Slow down; deliberate longer steps |
      | Back rounding during set-down | Hinge at hips, not spine |
      | Grip failing early | Use chalk or straps (if training pure carry, no straps) |

      **Distances and loads:**

      - Beginner: 20-30m with light load
      - Intermediate: 30-40m with moderate load
      - Advanced: 40m+ with heavy load
      - Competition strongman: 20-30m for time with max load

  - level: 3
    title: "The Biomechanics of the Farmer Carry"
    body: |
      The farmer carry is a loaded gait pattern. It trains the body under asymmetric loads while walking -- a pattern that shows up everywhere in real life and sport.

      **Muscles involved:**

      - **Grip:** forearm flexors, extensors, and intrinsic hand muscles work maximally
      - **Traps (upper and mid):** shoulder stability under load
      - **Core:** obliques, rectus abdominis, and erector spinae brace against the load
      - **Legs:** quads, glutes, hamstrings, calves drive the gait
      - **Feet/ankles:** intrinsic foot muscles stabilize each step

      **Force demands:**

      - Load per hand: 30-60% of bodyweight for intermediate athletes
      - Grip force required: near maximal
      - Trunk compression: significant, requiring bracing
      - Ground contact forces: 1.5-2x bodyweight per step

      **The "loaded gait" effect:**

      Unlike static carries (holding weight in place), the farmer carry adds a walk cycle. This introduces:

      - Alternating single-leg support -- each step is a mini unilateral hold
      - Lateral sway control -- core resists side-to-side movement
      - Cyclic loading -- grip, posture, and gait all degrade under sustained load

      **Programming implications:**

      - Start with 20m sets to build grip endurance
      - Progress distance before load
      - Use straps only if grip is not the training goal
      - Rest 60-90 sec between sets to allow grip recovery
      - 4-6 sets per session, 1-2x per week

      **Testing benchmarks:**

      | Level | Load per hand (% BW) | Distance |
      |---|---|---|
      | Beginner | 25-30% | 20m |
      | Intermediate | 40-50% | 30-40m |
      | Advanced | 60-75% | 40m+ |

      A 180 lb athlete carrying 90 lbs per hand for 40m is intermediate-advanced.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Farmer Carry Science"
    body: |
      At the research level, loaded carries are studied as a hybrid between strength training and gait training, with specific adaptations to grip, trunk, and locomotor systems.

      **Grip physiology:**

      Grip strength is a strong predictor of overall strength and health outcomes. Farmer carries train:

      - **Crush grip:** finger flexion against resistance
      - **Support grip:** sustained holding under load
      - **Pinch grip:** finger-thumb opposition (indirectly, via handle shape)

      Adaptations from regular carry training:
      - Increased forearm muscle cross-sectional area
      - Increased tendon stiffness in the flexor tendons
      - Improved neuromuscular drive to hand muscles
      - Increased capillary density in the forearm

      **Trunk and spine:**

      During a carry, the spine is loaded axially and laterally. This produces:

      - Increased intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) via bracing
      - Enhanced core stiffness
      - Isometric endurance adaptations in the erector spinae
      - Adaptation of the deep spinal stabilizers (multifidus, transverse abdominis)

      Unlike a squat or deadlift, the carry loads the spine with sustained (not ballistic) forces -- a different adaptation profile.

      **Gait mechanics under load:**

      - Stride length decreases slightly (10-15%) compared to unloaded walking
      - Ground contact time increases
      - Vertical oscillation increases (the body bounces more)
      - Trunk lean: maintained upright through active extension

      **Advanced programming models:**

      **1. Distance-based (endurance focus):**
      - 5-6 sets of 40m, 60-90 sec rest
      - Moderate loads (40-50% BW per hand)
      - Trains grip endurance and postural stamina

      **2. Load-based (strength focus):**
      - 3-4 sets of 20m, 2-3 min rest
      - Heavy loads (60-80% BW per hand)
      - Trains max grip strength and trunk stability

      **3. Time-based (competition focus):**
      - 2-3 sets of 60-90 sec max carry
      - Moderate loads
      - Trains grip endurance to failure

      **4. Unilateral variations:**
      - Single-arm carries (suitcase carry) -- adds anti-lateral flexion demand
      - Overhead carries -- adds shoulder stability demand

      **Loading variations:**

      | Variation | Load | Primary Adaptation |
      |---|---|---|
      | Standard farmer carry | Moderate-heavy bilateral | General grip + trunk |
      | Suitcase carry | Moderate unilateral | Anti-lateral flexion core |
      | Front-rack carry | Moderate | Anterior core, upper back |
      | Overhead carry | Light-moderate | Shoulder stability, core |
      | Trap bar carry | Heavy | Maximal grip and full-body |

      **Testing and benchmarks:**

      Eliteness in strongman typically requires:
      - 100+ kg per hand for 20m (heavyweight)
      - Sustained carries for 60+ sec with moderate loads
      - Time under tension exceeding 90 sec per set

      **Programming takeaways for advanced athletes:**

      - Alternate heavy (short distance) and light (long distance) sessions
      - Train each grip variation (crush, support, pinch) at least 1x per week
      - Use chalk for competition-style work; use straps only when grip is not the goal
      - Combine with single-arm and overhead variations for full-spectrum grip development
      - Deload grip work every 4-6 weeks -- forearm tendons need more recovery than muscle
      - If grip stalls, add supplementary finger and wrist work (plate pinches, wrist curls)

# Farmer Carry

Pick up something heavy in each hand. Walk. That's it. It's the simplest and most brutal grip and trunk builder in the weight room.
