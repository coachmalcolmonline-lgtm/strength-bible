  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Press"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Stance: hip width
      - Hands just outside shoulders
      - Bar resting on the "shelf" created by the shoulders
      - Elbows down and in front of the bar
      - Tight midsection, ribs locked down
      - Thumbs around the bar (closed grip)

      **Execution:**
      - Take a breath, brace
      - Press bar straight up
      - Head pulls back out of the way
      - Bar passes close to the face
      - Once bar clears, head returns to neutral
      - Lock out overhead: active shoulders, elbows straight
      - Return to shoulders under control

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Good setup
      - Constant tightness in the midsection, ribs locked down
      - Overhead and active shoulders at the top
      - Bar travels straight up

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Bar forward of frontal plane | Press up and pull back on bar |
      | Leaning back, ribs sticking out | Tighten abs, suck rib cage down |
      | Passive shoulders or bent elbows | Cue "Press up! Shoulders into ears" |
      | Bar arcs out around face | Pull head back out of the way; check elbows aren't too low in setup |

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming the Press"
    body: |
      **Loading:**
      - Bodyweight press = solid beginner standard for a trained athlete.
      - Press typically progresses slower than squat or deadlift. Upper body strength builds more slowly.
      - Frequency: 2× per week is common, often as part of a push/pull structure.

      **Role in programming:**
      - Direct upper body strength
      - Core and trunk stability (press requires whole-body bracing)
      - Transfer to push press, push jerk, and Olympic lifts

      **Press vs. bench:**
      - Bench press loads heavier, but the press is more functional.
      - The press demands core bracing, full-body tension, and true overhead strength.
      - Most athletes should press regularly even if they bench.

      **Progressions:**
      - Strict press → push press → push jerk
      - Each stage adds velocity and load
      - Push press allows ~30% more weight than strict press
      - Push jerk allows ~30% more than push press

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Press Physiology"
    body: |
      **Mechanics:**
      - The press is a closed-chain, full-body lift with the ground as the anchor point.
      - Force is transferred from legs (stabilization), through hips and trunk (core), to shoulders and arms.
      - Core is primary stabilizer. Lumbar hyperextension is the most common failure pattern when core fails.

      **Trunk mechanics:**
      - The rectus abdominis, obliques, and transverse abdominis all contribute to stabilizing the trunk under load.
      - The Valsalva maneuver is standard during heavy presses.
      - The "ribs down" cue prevents lumbar hyperextension and rib flare.

      **Muscle activation:**
      - Deltoids (anterior, lateral) — primary movers
      - Triceps — elbow extension
      - Upper pectorals — assist during mid-range
      - Serratus anterior — upward rotation of scapula
      - Rotator cuff — head of humerus stabilization

      **Programming:**
      - Press benefits from lower volume, higher intensity work.
      - Frequency of 1–2× per week is often optimal.
      - Press responds well to linear progression for novices.
      - Advanced lifters need periodized progression, often integrated with push press and jerk work.
