# The Floor Wiper

She wanted hanging wipers. She was going to have hanging wipers.

The first time she saw them, a senior did ten straight in front of the whole team -- hanging from the bar, legs out, sweeping side to side like windshield wipers in a storm. It looked like flying. It looked like something you either could or couldn't do.

She couldn't. Not even close.

She hung from the bar for eight seconds before her grip failed. Her legs barely came off the ground. The senior looked over and said, kindly, "Start on the floor."

---

The floor wipers were stupid. That was her first thought.

Lying on her back, hands under her hips, legs together and straight, sweeping them side to side like a metronome. It felt like nothing. It looked like nothing. It was, by any reasonable measure, an easier version of the thing she wanted to do.

"I'm not getting stronger doing this," she told her coach.

"You're not getting stronger doing nothing," he said. "You're getting the pattern."

She didn't understand what he meant.

---

Six weeks later, she still didn't have hanging wipers. She had:
- Floor wipers for 3 sets of 10, controlled
- A 25-second dead hang
- A hanging knee tuck she could barely do for 3 reps

None of it looked impressive. None of it looked like flying.

But it also didn't look like six weeks ago, when she couldn't hang for ten seconds.

The gremlin showed up around week four, on a Tuesday, during a conditioning circuit. She was doing floor wipers as the last station. Her legs were shaking. Her abs were on fire. The senior was hanging from the bar doing actual wipers, and the gap between them felt permanent.

*You're wasting your time,* the gremlin said. *You'll never do real wipers. You're just doing baby exercises forever while everyone else flies.*

She almost believed it. She almost stopped.

---

Here's what saved her: the notebook.

Every session, she wrote down the same things. Floor wipers, 3x10. Dead hang, 25 seconds. Hanging knee tuck, 3 reps. Same order. Same rest. Same time of day. It felt obsessive. It felt like she was just going through the motions.

But the notebook told her something the gremlin couldn't.

Week 1: dead hang 8 seconds.
Week 3: dead hang 15 seconds.
Week 5: dead hang 22 seconds.
Week 6: dead hang 25 seconds.

The gremlin said she wasn't progressing. The notebook said she was.

She learned something in that moment that she couldn't have learned any other way: **the gremlin lies.** It shows you the gap between where you are and where you want to be, and it hides the progress you've already made. The notebook doesn't.

---

The next session, she added one rep to the floor wipers. Not because she felt stronger -- because the notebook said she was.

A week later, she added two more reps.

A week after that, she added a third set.

Progress was slow. It was supposed to be slow. That was the point.

---

The first day she did a real hanging wiper, she did three.

She hung from the bar, pulled her legs to horizontal, and swept them side to side like the senior had done all those months ago. Three reps. Clean. Controlled.

She dropped off the bar and looked at her coach. He nodded, not surprised.

"I did it," she said.

"You did it," he said. "But you didn't start here. You started on the floor."

---

The floor wiper was never the point. The floor wiper was the rung.

Every movement has a ladder. The floor wiper was rung one. The hanging wiper was rung five. There was no way to skip from one to five -- the ladder doesn't work that way. But there was also no way to stay at rung one forever. Because she kept climbing.

The gremlin didn't go away. It never does. The gremlin lives in the gap between where you are and where you want to be. But she learned something that made the gremlin quieter:

The gremlin wants you to compare yourself to the person at the top of the ladder.

The notebook wants you to compare yourself to the person you were last week.

Compare to last week. The notebook wins every time.

---

She does hanging wipers now. Not many. Not fast. But real ones.

And on the days when they feel impossible -- when the bar feels like it's pulling her arms out of their sockets, when her legs won't lift, when the gremlin shows up and tells her she's wasting her time --

She doesn't argue with the gremlin. She doesn't try to out-talk it.

She opens the notebook.

And she reads what she wrote down last week.

Then she gets on the bar and does it anyway.

---

**The lessons:**

- **Scale deliberately.** The floor wiper is not "less than" the hanging wiper. It's the rung below it. Both are on the same ladder.
- **Standardize everything.** Same movements, same conditions, same notebook. This is how progress becomes visible.
- **The gremlin lies.** It shows you the gap and hides the progress. The notebook corrects the record.
- **"Don't want to" vs. "can't right now."** The gremlin says "don't want to." Your body says "can't right now." They feel similar. They are not similar.
- **Progress is slow. Slow is the point.** Six weeks to add a few reps. Eight months to do three real wipers. That's the timeline. Anyone who tells you differently is selling something.
- **The point is the ladder, not the top rung.** You will always be somewhere on the ladder. There is no finish line. There is only the next rung.

---

*Based on the arc of countless athletes who started on the floor and finished on the bar.*
