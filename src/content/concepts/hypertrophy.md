  - level: 1
    title: "What Is Hypertrophy?"
    body: |
      Hypertrophy means your muscles get bigger. Not fatter — bigger. The fibers themselves grow.

      Your body builds muscle when you ask it to do more than it's used to, then give it food and rest to recover. Lift something challenging, eat enough protein, sleep well, repeat. Over months, the muscle grows.

      Three things make hypertrophy happen:

      1. **Mechanical tension** — heavy or hard sets that challenge the muscle fibers
      2. **Metabolic stress** — the burn you feel during higher-rep work
      3. **Muscle damage** — small tears that get repaired bigger and stronger

      All three work. Most programs combine them.

      **Hypertrophy is not the same as strength.** A bigger muscle can usually lift more, but strength also comes from how well your nervous system recruits those muscles. That's why powerlifters and bodybuilders train differently.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Train for Hypertrophy"
    body: |
      Hypertrophy responds to **volume** more than intensity. That's the biggest shift from strength training.

      **The numbers that matter:**

      | Variable | Hypertrophy Range | Why |
      |---|---|---|
      | Load | 65–85% 1RM | Heavy enough to challenge, light enough for volume |
      | Reps | 6–12 per set | The classic "hypertrophy rep range" |
      | Sets per muscle group per week | 10–20 hard sets | Research supports this range for most athletes |
      | Rest between sets | 60–120 seconds | Shorter than strength, longer than you think |
      | Frequency | 2× per week per muscle | Splits the volume, better recovery |

      **Two other rep ranges also work:**

      - **Heavy (3–6 reps at 85%+):** builds strength, adds size slower
      - **Light (15–30 reps at 50–65%):** builds size via metabolic stress if taken close to failure

      The "best" rep range is the one you can accumulate quality volume in without wrecking your joints.

      **Proximity to failure matters more than load.** A set of 10 at RPE 7 (3 reps left) does less than a set of 10 at RPE 9 (1 rep left). Take your last set of each exercise close to failure — 1–3 reps in reserve (RIR) — and you'll grow.

  - level: 3
    title: "The Science of Muscle Growth"
    body: |
      Hypertrophy happens at the cellular level through three mechanisms, each triggering overlapping signaling pathways.

      **1. Mechanical tension**
      High-threshold motor units are recruited when load exceeds ~80% 1RM OR when reps are taken near failure. The mechanical force on the sarcolemma, cytoskeleton, and integrins triggers **mTORC1** — the master regulator of muscle protein synthesis (MPS). MPS is elevated for 24–72 hours post-training.

      **2. Metabolic stress**
      Moderate-load, higher-rep work accumulates metabolites: lactate, hydrogen ions, inorganic phosphate, and reactive oxygen species. This drives cellular swelling and additional signaling for growth. It's the "pump" — and it's not just cosmetic.

      **3. Muscle damage**
      Microtears in the sarcomere and connective tissue trigger an inflammatory repair response. Satellite cells activate, proliferate, and fuse into existing fibers, donating nuclei. Moderate damage drives adaptation. Excessive damage (e.g., 100-rep sets to total failure) impairs recovery without adding growth.

      **The dose-response is an inverted U.** Too little stimulus, no growth. Too much, no growth (you can't recover). The sweet spot shifts with training age, sleep, nutrition, and stress.

      **Practical implications:**

      - **Train each muscle 2× per week.** Splits the volume and gives each session the recovery it needs to drive adaptation.
      - **Use 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week.** Below 10, growth is suboptimal. Above 20, diminishing returns unless recovery is elite.
      - **Mix rep ranges.** Heavy sets (4–6) drive tension; moderate sets (8–12) drive tension + metabolic stress; light sets (15–20) drive metabolic stress. Use all three across a mesocycle.
      - **Progressive overload still applies.** More weight, more reps, more sets, or better technique. Without overload, no adaptation.

  - level: 4
    title: "Molecular Signaling & Individual Variation"
    body: |
      At the research level, hypertrophy is a systems problem with massive individual variation.

      **Signaling cascades:**

      - **mTORC1** — activated by mechanical load, amino acid availability (especially leucine), and insulin. Drives translation initiation and ribosomal biogenesis.
      - **MAPK** — activated by mechanical stress and metabolic stress. Modulates gene expression for structural proteins.
      - **Calcineurin / NFAT** — activated by calcium flux and sustained low-frequency stimulation. Drives slow-fiber phenotype shift.
      - **AMPK** — activated by energy stress. Inhibits mTORC1 — this is why excessive cardio can blunt hypertrophy.

      **Myonuclear permanence:** Satellite cells donate nuclei to muscle fibers during hypertrophy. These myonuclei persist through detraining, creating a "muscle memory" effect that accelerates re-growth. This is why detrained athletes regain size faster than true beginners.

      **Dose-response meta-analyses (Schoenfeld, Refalo, Pelland, et al.):**

      - Volume is the strongest predictor of hypertrophy, with a dose-response relationship up to ~20 sets per muscle per week in most studies.
      - Beyond that, diminishing returns vary widely — some advanced lifters tolerate 30+ sets, others plateau at 12.
      - Proximity to failure matters, but not to failure itself — 1–3 RIR maximizes volume while limiting fatigue.
      - Frequency of 2× per week outperforms 1× for the same weekly volume in most meta-analyses.

      **Individual variation:**

      - **Responders vs. non-responders:** ~10–20% of athletes show minimal hypertrophy even under well-controlled training. Genetic variation in mTOR sensitivity, satellite cell function, and androgen receptor density explains much of this.
      - **Muscle-specific response:** Different muscles grow at different rates in the same athlete. Calves are notoriously slow; quads and lats usually respond fast.
      - **Recovery capacity:** Sleep, protein intake (1.6–2.2 g/kg/day), energy availability, and life stress all gate the adaptation.

      **Programming takeaways:**

      - Individualize volume based on recovery and progress, not an absolute number.
      - Prioritize sleep and protein before adding more sets.
      - Deload every 4–8 weeks — accumulated fatigue masks adaptation.
      - Track progress with standardized measurements (same time of day, same conditions) to separate real growth from noise.

# Hypertrophy

Hypertrophy is muscle growth — the fibers themselves getting larger. It's driven by mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage, and it responds most strongly to weekly volume per muscle group.
