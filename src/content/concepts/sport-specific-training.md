  - level: 1
    title: "What Is Sport-Specific Training?"
    body: |
      Sport-specific training means training the physical qualities that make you better at YOUR sport -- not just getting generally stronger.

      A lineman and a receiver both play football, but they need different things. A sprinter and a marathoner both run, but they train completely differently. A pitcher and a catcher both play baseball, but their strength needs are opposite.

      **Sport-specific training answers three questions:**

      1. **What does my sport actually demand?** Sprints, jumps, throws, tackles, endurance, change of direction?
      2. **What physical qualities support those demands?** Power, strength, speed, mobility, work capacity?
      3. **What can I do in the weight room that transfers to the field?**

      The general rule: **the further you are from your season, the more general your training. The closer you are to your season, the more specific.**

      In the off-season, you build a big general base -- squat, deadlift, press, carry. In-season, you maintain strength and stay healthy. Pre-season, you sharpen sport-specific qualities.

      **Sport-specific training is not "sport practice in the weight room."** You don't get better at baseball by swinging a bat in the gym. You get better at baseball by getting stronger, faster, and more powerful, then applying those qualities on the field.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Program Sport-Specific Training"
    body: |
      Every sport has a profile: what energy systems it uses, what movements it demands, and what physical qualities predict success. Your job is to match your training to that profile.

      **The four-phase model:**

      | Phase | Focus | Volume | Intensity |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Off-season | Build general strength + size | High | Moderate |
      | Pre-season | Sport-specific power + speed | Moderate | High |
      | In-season | Maintain strength, stay healthy | Low | Moderate |
      | Post-season | Rest + rebuild | Very low | Low |

      **Key principles:**

      1. **Strength is the base.** Every sport benefits from stronger legs, hips, and trunk. Build this first.
      2. **Power comes second.** Once strong, learn to express that strength quickly.
      3. **Speed and agility are specific.** Sprinting and change of direction must be trained with sprinting and change of direction.
      4. **Conditioning matches the sport.** A soccer player runs for 90 minutes; a lineman runs 5 seconds at a time. Train accordingly.
      5. **In-season is maintenance, not development.** You can't get stronger during the season -- you're trying not to lose what you built.
      6. **Recovery is training.** Sleep, food, and stress management determine whether your work shows up on the field.

      **Weekly structure in-season (most sports):**

      | Day | Session |
      |---|---|
      | Mon | Lift + practice |
      | Tue | Practice |
      | Wed | Lift + practice |
      | Thu | Practice |
      | Fri | Practice or game |
      | Sat | Game |
      | Sun | Rest |

      Lifts: 2 sessions per week, 45-60 min each. Low volume, moderate intensity. Focus on the big lifts (squat, deadlift, press, clean) plus 2-3 accessories. No soreness-inducing work.

      **The in-season rule:** never train so hard you can't perform in practice or a game. If your legs are dead from a squat session, you can't sprint. If you can't sprint, you can't play.

  - level: 3
    title: "The Science of Sport-Specific Transfer"
    body: |
      Sport-specific programming is grounded in the principle of **specificity** -- the body adapts to the specific demands placed on it.

      **Specificity principles:**

      1. **SAID principle:** Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands. Your body adapts to exactly what you ask of it, nothing more.
      2. **Transfer of training:** The more similar the training is to the sport movement, the greater the transfer.
      3. **Dynamic correspondence:** Yuri Verkhoshansky's framework for matching training to sport. Five criteria:
         - Amplitude and direction of movement
         - Accentuated region of force production
         - Dynamics of effort
         - Rate and timing of maximum force
         - Regime of muscular work (concentric, eccentric, isometric)

      **Force-velocity profile of common sports:**

      | Sport | Dominant Quality | Primary Energy System |
      |---|---|---|
      | Sprinting | Max velocity, acceleration | ATP-PC |
      | Distance running | Aerobic endurance | Oxidative |
      | Basketball | Repeated jumps, change of direction | Mixed |
      | Soccer | Repeated sprints, aerobic base | Oxidative + glycolytic |
      | Football (lineman) | Explosive power, max strength | ATP-PC |
      | Football (skill) | Acceleration, change of direction | ATP-PC + glycolytic |
      | Baseball (pitcher) | Rotational power, arm strength | ATP-PC |
      | Baseball (catcher) | Repeated squats, hip mobility | ATP-PC + glycolytic |
      | Wrestling | Strength endurance, grip | Glycolytic + oxidative |
      | Volleyball | Vertical jump, repeated efforts | ATP-PC |
      | Lacrosse | Repeated sprints, change of direction | Mixed |

      **Position-specific demands:**

      Even within one sport, positions differ. In football:
      - Linemen: max strength, explosive power, body mass
      - Skill positions (WR, DB, RB): acceleration, change of direction, top speed
      - Linebackers: combination of the two

      In baseball:
      - Pitchers: rotational power, shoulder health, repeated high-intensity efforts
      - Catchers: hip mobility, repeated squatting, upper-body durability
      - Outfielders: acceleration, sprint speed, arm strength

      **Programming implications:**

      - Identify the dominant quality for your position -- train it first
      - Build general strength in the off-season, sport-specific power in the pre-season
      - In-season: maintain what you built with minimal volume
      - Use testing to verify transfer -- if squat goes up but 40yd doesn't, transfer is broken
      - Recovery must match training volume -- in-season athletes need more sleep and food than in the off-season

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Sport-Specific Programming"
    body: |
      At the research level, sport-specific training is a transfer problem with measurable input variables and specific performance outputs.

      **The transfer equation:**

      Sport performance = f(force production x velocity x skill x fatigue resistance)

      Each of those variables is trainable. The art is in knowing which to prioritize for a given athlete, position, and phase.

      **Force-velocity profiling per athlete:**

      Using a linear position transducer or app-based bar tracking, plot each athlete's force-velocity curve. Identify whether they are:

      - **Force-deficient:** high velocity, low force. Needs more heavy strength work.
      - **Velocity-deficient:** high force, low velocity. Needs more speed work.
      - **Balanced:** both qualities developing together. Continue current programming.

      Elite practitioners use this to individualize training within a team setting. Two athletes on the same team might get different programs based on their profiles.

      **The in-season problem:**

      In-season athletes are caught between two demands:

      1. **Sport practice** loads the body 10-20 hours per week
      2. **Strength training** adds more load on top

      The result: total training stress often exceeds recovery capacity. Common outcomes:
      - Performance decline mid-season
      - Injury rates spike
      - Athletes burn out

      The solution: **in-season strength training is a maintenance stimulus, not a development stimulus.** Research (Hoffman et al., 1990; Rønnestad et al., 2011) shows that:

      - 1-2 sessions per week at 80-90% intensity maintains strength for 8-12 weeks
      - Higher volumes add fatigue without adding performance
      - Compound lifts (squat, deadlift, clean, press) preserve performance better than isolation work

      **Minimal effective dose for maintenance:**

      | Lift | Frequency | Volume | Intensity |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Squat | 1-2x/week | 2-3 sets | 80-85% |
      | Deadlift | 1x/week | 2-3 sets | 80-85% |
      | Press | 1-2x/week | 2-3 sets | 80-85% |
      | Olympic derivative | 1x/week | 3-4 sets | 70-80% |
      | Sport-specific power | 1x/week | 3-4 sets | Light-moderate |

      This is the minimum required to maintain. Anything less loses strength; anything more adds fatigue.

      **The taper:**

      For a key game or competition, taper training volume 3-5 days out:
      - Cut volume 40-60%
      - Maintain intensity (same weights, fewer reps)
      - Final lift session 2-3 days before competition
      - Focus on sleep, food, and mental prep

      **Return-to-play after injury:**

      Athletes returning from injury need a step-wise progression back to sport-specific training. Steps:

      1. **Restore range of motion** (full active ROM on the injured joint)
      2. **Restore strength** (return to 90%+ of pre-injury lift numbers)
      3. **Restore power** (jumps, throws, sprints at moderate intensity)
      4. **Restore sport-specific movement** (cutting, landing, sport-specific drills)
      5. **Full return** (game intensity)

      Skipping steps increases re-injury risk. Each step should be cleared before moving on.

      **Programming takeaways for advanced athletes:**

      - Profile force-velocity per athlete, not per team
      - In-season is maintenance, not development
      - Use the minimal effective dose for in-season work
      - Taper 3-5 days before key competitions
      - Return from injury in steps, not leaps
      - Track performance, not just gym numbers -- the field is the final test

# Sport-Specific Training

Sport-specific training means training the qualities that make you better at your sport -- not just getting generally stronger. Match the training to the sport's demands, and adjust by phase.
