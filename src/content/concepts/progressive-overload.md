    title: "What Is Progressive Overload?"
    body: |
      Your body is a machine that adapts. When you give it a challenge it hasn't faced before, it rebuilds itself to handle that challenge next time.

      If you never increase the challenge — same weight, same reps, same speed, forever — your body has no reason to change. You plateau. You stall. You get bored.

      **Progressive overload** is the answer. It means: gradually, over time, give your body a little more than it's used to.

      More weight. More reps. Better technique. Less rest. Any of these — as long as it's a little more than last time.

  - level: 2
    title: "How Progressive Overload Actually Works"
    body: |
      Progressive overload isn't just "add weight." It's any of these:

      | Variable | What It Means | Example |
      |---|---|---|
      | Load | Add weight to the bar | 135 → 140 lbs |
      | Volume | Add reps or sets | 3x5 → 3x6 |
      | Density | Same work, less time | 5 rounds in 12 min → 10 min |
      | Technique | Better range of motion, control, bar path | Above parallel → below parallel |
      | Frequency | Train more often | 2x/week → 3x/week |

      Critical rule: Never sacrifice technique for load. A sloppy 200-lb squat is not progressive overload — it's just a heavier mistake.

  - level: 3
    title: "The Physiology of Overload"
    body: |
      Progressive overload drives adaptation through three primary mechanisms:

      1. Mechanical tension — force on muscle fibers during contraction. High-threshold motor units recruited at ~80% 1RM or near failure.
      2. Metabolic stress — metabolite accumulation during moderate-load, higher-rep work. Drives cellular signaling for hypertrophy.
      3. Muscle damage — microtears triggering inflammatory repair response. Optimal, not maximal.

      Neural adaptations dominate early. Beginners add load almost every session. As training age increases, neural gains plateau, and progress depends on structural adaptations (hypertrophy, tendon stiffness, bone density).

      Autoregulation (RPE/RIR) adjusts daily load based on readiness.

  - level: 4
    title: "Molecular & Systems-Level View"
    body: |
      Progressive overload is transduced at the cellular level through mechanotransduction: mechanical forces trigger signaling cascades including mTORC1, MAPK, and calcineurin pathways. Downstream effects:

      - Myofibrillar protein synthesis (MPS) — elevated 24-72 hours post-training
      - Satellite cell activation — proliferation and fusion into fibers
      - Connective tissue remodeling — collagen synthesis, tendon CSA increase, bone density (Wolff's Law)
      - Neural plasticity — motor cortex representation changes

      Dose-response is nonlinear. Inverted-U relationship. Optimal zone shifts with training age, nutrition, sleep, stress, genetics.

      Periodization manages fatigue and potentiates adaptation. Meta-analyses (Rhea et al. 2003; Peterson et al. 2005) support periodized over non-periodized training for 1RM strength.

      Autoregulation using RPE/RIR (Zourdos et al. 2016) produces comparable or superior outcomes to fixed-percentage programming.
