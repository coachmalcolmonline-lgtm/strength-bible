    title: "What Is a Deload?"
    body: |
      A deload is a planned week of lighter training. You don't stop training. You back off — less weight, fewer sets, or both.

      Why? Because training makes you tired before it makes you stronger. The adaptation happens during recovery, not during the workout.

      If you never back off, you accumulate fatigue faster than you recover. Progress stalls. You feel sluggish. You get hurt.

      A deload lets your body catch up. Then you come back stronger.

  - level: 2
    title: "When and How to Deload"
    body: |
      **When to deload:**

      **Planned deloads** — every 4–8 weeks, depending on training age and volume:

      - Beginners: every 6–8 weeks
      - Intermediates: every 4–6 weeks
      - Advanced: every 3–4 weeks (or more often with high volume)

      **Reactive deloads** — when signals say you need one:

      - RPE creeping 2+ points above target for 3+ sessions
      - Sleep quality dropping, motivation dropping
      - Joint pain that doesn't resolve with warm-up
      - Persistent fatigue that doesn't clear with a rest day
      - No progress for 2+ weeks

      **How to deload:**

      Standard deload:

      - Reduce volume 40–60% (fewer sets)
      - Reduce intensity 10–20% (lighter weights)
      - Keep RPE 5–7 (not 8–10)
      - Same exercises, same frequency
      - 5–7 days

      **What NOT to do:**

      - Don't take the whole week off unless you're sick or injured
      - Don't test maxes
      - Don't add new exercises
      - Don't try to "make up" for it next week

      Deloads are not lost time. They're the reason progress continues.

  - level: 3
    title: "Supercompensation & Fatigue Management"
    body: |
      **Supercompensation model:** Training creates fatigue and stimulus. If you recover properly, you return to baseline and then exceed it — this is supercompensation. If you train again before recovery completes, you dig a fatigue hole. If you keep digging, you overreach, then overtrain.

      **Two-factor model (fitness-fatigue):** Every session produces both fitness and fatigue. Fatigue decays faster than fitness. Deloads let fatigue decay while fitness remains, resulting in a net increase in performance.

      **When to deload — signal-based framework:**

      | Marker | Green | Yellow | Red |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | RPE vs. target | At or below | 1 point above | 2+ points above |
      | Sleep | 7+ hours, refreshing | 6-7 hours | Under 6 or unrefreshing |
      | Motivation | High | Neutral | Low |
      | Joint pain | None | Mild, resolves with warm-up | Persistent |
      | Progress | PRs happening | Plateau | Regression |

      **Yellow = reactive deload soon. Red = deload now.**

      **Types of deloads:**

      - **Full deload:** Reduce both volume and intensity
      - **Volume deload:** Keep intensity, cut sets
      - **Intensity deload:** Keep volume, lighter weights
      - **Movement deload:** Same weight, but change exercises (block pulls instead of deadlifts, etc.)

      **Programming implication:** A well-designed program has deloads built in. If you're constantly needing reactive deloads, the program is wrong — either too much volume, too much intensity, or not enough recovery.

  - level: 4
    title: "Physiology & Research"
    body: |
      **Physiological rationale:**

      - **Neuromuscular fatigue:** High-intensity training reduces motor unit firing rates and increases the time to peak force. Recovery restores these.
      - **Endocrine recovery:** Testosterone, cortisol, IGF-1 fluctuate with training stress. Chronic elevation of cortisol suppresses adaptation.
      - **Connective tissue repair:** Tendons and ligaments adapt slower than muscle. Deloads give them time to catch up.
      - **CNS recovery:** The central nervous system fatigues with heavy load. Time is required for full restoration of rate coding and intermuscular coordination.

      **Research summary:**

      - Deloads are recommended in virtually all periodization literature (Stone, Fleck, Kraemer).
      - Meta-analyses on periodization (Rhea et al. 2003) show better outcomes with planned deloads vs. no deloads.
      - Overreaching (short-term fatigue) followed by deload can produce "supercompensation" and PRs (Kreider et al., Meeusen et al. on overtraining syndrome).
      - Overreaching beyond 2–3 weeks without deload increases risk of overtraining syndrome (OTS), which can require weeks to months to recover from.

      **Advanced deload strategies:**

      - **Block periodization:** Deload weeks built into every block.
      - **Conjugate method:** Rotate max effort, dynamic effort, and repetition effort to manage fatigue without full deload.
      - **Auto-regulated deloads:** Deload triggered by RPE creep, HRV drop, or velocity loss.
      - **Taper for competition:** Shorter, more focused, emphasizes reduced volume while maintaining intensity.

      **The overtraining trap:** Athletes who confuse "tired" with "lazy" push through fatigue. Chronic overreaching becomes overtraining, which can cost 3–6 months of progress. Deloads are not weakness — they're how the strong stay strong.
