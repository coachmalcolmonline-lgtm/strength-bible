  - level: 2
    title: "Technique Before Load"
    body: |
      Technique Priority isn't a suggestion. It's a rule you follow until it becomes automatic.

      The hierarchy is:

      1. **Master the movement** at bodyweight or light load
      2. **Add load only when technique holds** at the current weight
      3. **Progress the load slowly** while technique stays clean
      4. **Stop when technique breaks** — that's your session's limit

      What "technique holds" means:

      - Full range of motion
      - Lumbar curve maintained
      - Weight in the right place (heels for squats, mid-foot for pulls)
      - Bar path is clean
      - No rushing, no bouncing, no compensating

      If technique breaks, you're done with that lift for the day. Reduce the load, or move on. Don't chase reps with broken form.

  - level: 3
    title: "Why Technique Is Load-Bearing"
    body: |
      In strength training, technique is not just aesthetic. It's mechanical.

      **Force transfer:** Good technique aligns the joints so force produced by muscles is transferred efficiently to the bar. Sloppy technique leaks force — you produce it, but it doesn't reach the barbell.

      **Joint health:** Proper alignment keeps load distributed through the structure the joint was designed to handle. Poor alignment concentrates force on tissues that aren't built for it — tendons, ligaments, cartilage, discs.

      **Motor learning:** The nervous system learns whatever you repeat. If you practice sloppy reps, you're training your body to be sloppy. Those patterns become default. Fixing them later takes 3–5× as long as learning them correctly the first time.

      **Long-term ceilings:** Athletes who learn to grind through poor technique hit a ceiling fast. Once the weight gets heavy enough, the compensations fail and progress stops. Athletes who built technique first keep progressing for years.

      **Rate of Force Development (RFD):** Clean technique allows faster, more powerful reps. Slop is slow.

  - level: 4
    title: "Neuromuscular & Biomechanical Depth"
    body: |
      **Motor unit recruitment & coordination:** Clean technique maximizes the body's ability to recruit high-threshold motor units in the correct sequence. Intermuscular coordination — the timing between agonist, antagonist, and stabilizer muscles — is what separates trained lifters from untrained.

      **Rate coding:** Trained lifters fire motor units faster, not just more. This takes thousands of clean repetitions to develop.

      **Motor engrams:** Repeated clean reps build a "motor engram" — a neural template for the movement. Once established, it becomes automatic. Once a bad engram is established, it must be overwritten before good technique can become default. This is why early-stage athletes should never train to failure on new movements.

      **Joint capsule & connective tissue adaptation:** Technique drives load through the correct structures. Tendons and ligaments adapt to load in the direction they're loaded. Misdirected load causes micro-trauma that accumulates into chronic injury.

      **Specificity principle:** Adaptations are specific to the movement performed. If you practice a bad version of the squat, you get stronger at the bad version. That strength doesn't transfer to the sport you care about.

      **Periodization implication:** Technique work should be present in every phase. Even during max-strength blocks, warm-up sets and technique-focused volume keep the pattern sharp.

      **Injury cost modeling:** A single technique-related injury can cost 6–12 months of progress. The return on investment for slow, technique-focused progression dwarfs the short-term gain from chasing weight.
