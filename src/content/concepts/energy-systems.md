    title: "Where Does Energy Come From?"
    body: |
      Every movement you make — a sprint, a squat, a breath — uses energy. Your body has three ways to make that energy. They turn on at different times and for different durations.

      Think of them like gears on a bike:

      - **Gear 1 (ATP-PC):** Instant energy. Lasts ~10 seconds. Powers a max sprint or a heavy single.
      - **Gear 2 (Glycolytic):** Fast energy. Lasts ~30 seconds to 2 minutes. Powers a 400m sprint or a hard set of 20 reps.
      - **Gear 3 (Oxidative):** Slow, steady energy. Lasts minutes to hours. Powers a distance run or a long walk.

      You use all three, all day, in different proportions. A 100m sprint is almost all Gear 1. A marathon is almost all Gear 3. A basketball game is a mix — sprints, stops, jogging, back to sprints.

      **Why this matters:** if you want to get better at something, you train the gear that powers it.

  - level: 2
    title: "The Three Systems in Detail"
    body: |
      Each system has a job. Train the right one for the right goal.

      **1. ATP-PC (Phosphagen) System**

      - **Duration:** 0–10 seconds
      - **Fuel:** Creatine phosphate stored in muscle
      - **Powers:** Max sprints, heavy singles, jumps, throws
      - **Train it with:** 5–10 second max efforts, full recovery (3–5 min)
      - **Recovery time:** 2–5 minutes to fully restore

      **2. Glycolytic System**

      - **Duration:** 10 seconds – ~2 minutes
      - **Fuel:** Glucose/glycogen broken down without oxygen
      - **Powers:** 200–800m runs, hard 20-rep sets, most "conditioning"
      - **Byproduct:** Lactate + hydrogen ions (the "burn")
      - **Train it with:** 30 sec – 2 min efforts, partial recovery (1–3 min)
      - **Recovery time:** 30 min – several hours

      **3. Oxidative (Aerobic) System**

      - **Duration:** 2 minutes – hours
      - **Fuel:** Fat and glucose, with oxygen
      - **Powers:** Distance running, recovery between efforts, most daily activity
      - **Train it with:** Sustained 20–60+ min efforts at moderate intensity, or intervals
      - **Recovery time:** minimal — that's the point

      **The overlap:** all three run at once, always. What changes is the **dominant** system.

      **Training takeaways:**

      - If you want a faster 40-yard dash → train ATP-PC (short max sprints).
      - If you want to survive a hard 2-minute conditioning test → train glycolytic.
      - If you want to recover faster between sets, between sessions, and between plays → train oxidative.
      - Most athletes need all three. The mix depends on the sport.

  - level: 3
    title: "Energy System Physiology"
    body: |
      At the cellular level, energy systems are about how fast ATP (adenosine triphosphate) can be regenerated. ATP is the actual currency of cellular work — every muscle contraction burns ATP. The three systems differ in how quickly and for how long they can regenerate it.

      **ATP-PC (Phosphagen) System**

      - Reaction: PCr + ADP → ATP + Cr (via creatine kinase)
      - Rate: fastest — ATP regenerated in milliseconds
      - Capacity: smallest — muscle stores only ~5–10 seconds of PCr
      - Recovery: 70% restored in 30 seconds, ~95% in 3–5 minutes
      - Limiting factor: PCr availability

      **Glycolytic System**

      - Reaction: Glucose/glycogen → 2 ATP + 2 lactate (via ~10 enzymatic steps)
      - Rate: fast — ATP regenerated in seconds
      - Capacity: moderate — ~30 sec to 2 min at high intensity
      - Recovery: lactate cleared in 30–60 min (active recovery speeds this)
      - Limiting factor: H+ accumulation (acidosis) and glycogen depletion

      **Oxidative (Aerobic) System**

      - Reaction: Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA → Krebs cycle → electron transport chain → ~30–32 ATP per glucose
      - Rate: slow — ATP regenerated in seconds to minutes, but sustainable
      - Capacity: massive — hours, if substrate is available
      - Recovery: essentially none — the system is what recovers you
      - Limiting factors: oxygen delivery, mitochondrial density, fat oxidation rate

      **Practical implications:**

      - **PCr depletion is why max sprints need 3–5 min rest.** Train with full recovery if you want quality.
      - **Lactate is not the enemy.** It's a fuel, not a waste product. The "burn" comes from hydrogen ions, not lactate itself.
      - **The aerobic system drives recovery.** The stronger your aerobic base, the faster you recover between sets, between efforts, and between sessions.
      - **Training shifts the mix.** Endurance athletes have more mitochondria and capillaries. Sprinters have more PCr and glycolytic enzymes. Both adaptations are trainable.

      **Energy system development over time:**

      - **Weeks 1–3:** high volume, moderate intensity (build the aerobic base)
      - **Weeks 4–6:** introduce threshold work (glycolytic tolerance)
      - **Weeks 7–8:** sharpening (ATP-PC power, short max efforts)

      This is the standard periodization for energy system development in team sports.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Energy System Science"
    body: |
      At the research level, energy system training is a bioenergetics problem with measurable determinants and specific adaptations.

      **Bioenergetics measurements:**

      - **VO2max:** max rate of oxygen consumption (ml/kg/min). The ceiling for aerobic ATP production. Trained via intervals at 90–100% VO2max.
      - **Lactate threshold (LT1/LT2):** the intensity at which blood lactate starts accumulating faster than it clears. LT1 (~2 mmol/L) is where you can hold effort for hours; LT2 (~4 mmol/L) is where you can hold for ~30–60 min. Training near LT2 shifts it higher.
      - **Critical power / critical speed (CP/CS):** the highest intensity that can be sustained without fatiguing. Above CP, work is finite; below it, sustainable.
      - **W' (W-prime):** the finite work capacity above CP. Depleted during hard efforts; recovered with rest or low-intensity work.

      **Adaptations to aerobic training:**

      - Increased mitochondrial density and enzyme activity
      - Increased capillary density (improved oxygen delivery)
      - Increased stroke volume and cardiac output
      - Increased fat oxidation at higher intensities (glycogen sparing)
      - Increased lactate clearance rate

      **Adaptations to anaerobic training:**

      - Increased muscle PCr stores
      - Increased glycolytic enzyme activity (PFK, LDH)
      - Increased buffering capacity (H+ clearance)
      - Increased neuromuscular recruitment at high intensity

      **Training methods for each system:**

      **ATP-PC:**
      - 5–10 sec max efforts, 3–5 min rest, 4–8 reps per session
      - Examples: 30m sprint repeats, jump repeats, heavy single at 90%+ 1RM
      - Frequency: 2–3× per week

      **Glycolytic:**
      - 30 sec – 2 min hard efforts, 1–3 min rest, 4–10 reps
      - Examples: 400m repeats, 20-rep squat sets, hard rowing intervals
      - Frequency: 1–2× per week (recovery is demanding)

      **Oxidative:**
      - 20–60+ min sustained, or intervals 3–8 min at 80–90% max HR
      - Examples: tempo runs, cycling, rucking, steady erg work
      - Frequency: 3–5× per week (recovery is cheap)

      **Concurrent training interference:**

      The classic problem: doing both endurance and strength in the same block can blunt strength gains. Mechanism: AMPK activation by aerobic work inhibits mTORC1 (the strength/hypertrophy signal). Practical mitigations:

      - Separate sessions by 6+ hours, or on different days
      - Do strength first if same-day
      - Keep aerobic volume moderate in strength blocks
      - Periodize: aerobic base in off-season, strength in-season with maintenance aerobic

      **Programming takeaways:**

      - Build the aerobic base first. It recovers cheap and supports everything else.
      - Add threshold work second. Small doses go a long way.
      - Sharpen with ATP-PC work last. It's the tip of the pyramid.
      - Track HR, RPE, and pace to see system-specific adaptation.
      - Respect interference — don't stack hard aerobic and hard strength back to back.

# Energy Systems

Your body runs on three energy systems — ATP-PC (instant), glycolytic (fast), and oxidative (steady). Each powers a different type of effort. Train the one that fuels your sport.
