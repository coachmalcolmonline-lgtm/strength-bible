# Safety

Training is safe when done correctly and dangerous when done poorly. This page is not legal boilerplate. It's the real talk.

## The Three Rules

**1. Technique before load.**
Never add weight to a movement you can't perform correctly with lighter weight. A sloppy 200-lb squat is not progress. It's debt that comes due in the form of injury.

**2. Progressive, not heroic.**
Small increases over time. Not big jumps. Not maxing out every session. Not chasing PRs when you're tired. The athlete who adds 5 lbs a week for a year beats the athlete who adds 50 lbs once and gets hurt.

**3. Listen to your body.**
Sharp pain is a stop signal. Dull soreness is normal. Joint pain is not "just part of training." If something hurts in a way that feels wrong, stop. Rest. Reassess. See a professional if it doesn't resolve.

## When to Stop Immediately

- Sharp or shooting pain anywhere
- Dizziness, lightheadedness, or nausea that doesn't resolve
- Chest pain or difficulty breathing
- Sudden weakness on one side of the body
- Any joint that feels "wrong" during a lift

## When to See a Doctor

- Pain that lasts more than 3–5 days
- Swelling that doesn't go down
- Numbness or tingling that persists
- Any injury that prevents you from training normally for more than a week
- Dark or cola-colored urine after intense training (this can indicate rhabdomyolysis — a medical emergency)

## Rhabdomyolysis — The Rare but Real Risk

Rhabdo is a breakdown of muscle tissue that releases contents into the bloodstream and can damage the kidneys. It occurs rarely after extreme exercise, especially:

- First-time high-volume workouts
- Returning to training after a long layoff
- Intense eccentric work (negatives, high-rep bodyweight)
- Training in extreme heat while dehydrated

**Signs:** severe muscle pain, weakness, swelling, dark urine.
**Action:** Go to the emergency room. Do not wait.

## Gym Etiquette & Basic Safety

- Warm up before every session
- Use collars on loaded barbells
- Load and unload plates evenly
- Don't drop loaded bars unless it's a legitimate bail from a lift
- Rack your weights when done
- Ask for a spot when attempting near-maximal lifts
- Communicate with your training partners

## A Note on Training Alone

Training alone is fine for most sessions. For heavy squats, bench, or Olympic lifts, use safety pins or ask for a spot. Never max out alone without safeties.

## The Bottom Line

Training is one of the safest things you can do — when you do it right. Injuries happen when ego beats judgment. Don't let that happen to you.

If something doesn't feel right, it isn't. Stop. Fix it. Come back stronger.
