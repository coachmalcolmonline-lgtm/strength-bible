    title: "What Is the Pull-up?"
    body: |
      The pull-up is the upper body pulling equivalent of the push-up. You hang from a bar and pull your chin over it.

      It's hard. Most people can't do one the first time they try. That's normal.

      **Why it matters:**
      - Builds upper body pulling strength — the muscles most people neglect
      - Balances out all the pressing (bench, push-ups, presses)
      - Develops the lats, biceps, and grip
      - Essential for rock climbing, wrestling, and any sport that involves pulling

      The pull-up is a benchmark. When you can do 10 clean reps, you've built real upper body strength.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Pull-up"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Hang from the bar with an overhand grip (palms facing away)
      - Hands slightly wider than shoulders
      - Full hang — arms extended, shoulders active

      **Execution:**
      - Pull with your back and arms
      - Drive elbows down toward your ribs
      - Bring chin over the bar
      - Lower under control to a full hang

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Full range of motion (dead hang to chin over bar)
      - No swinging (strict) — for strict pull-ups
      - Controlled descent
      - Active shoulders at bottom (don't let them "unpack")

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Partial range of motion | Cue "Full extension at bottom, chin over bar at top" |
      | Kipping without control | Cue "Strict pull-ups first" |
      | Shoulders passive at bottom | Cue "Active shoulders" |
      | Using momentum | Cue "Control the descent" |
      | Chin not clearing the bar | Reduce reps, add negatives, build strength |

      **Progressions:**
      - Dead hang → scapular pulls → negatives → band-assisted → strict pull-up → weighted → one-arm progressions

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming & Variations"
    body: |
      **Training role:**
      - Pull-up is the primary vertical pulling movement.
      - Essential for balanced upper body development.
      - Trains grip, lats, biceps, rear delts, and scapular control.

      **Programming:**
      - 3–5 sets of 5–12 reps is standard.
      - For strength: weighted pull-ups (5×3–5 at 80%+ 1RM).
      - For volume: high-rep sets or EMOM (every minute on the minute).
      - For skill: variations (archer, L-sit, typewriter, one-arm).

      **Benchmarks:**
      - 1 strict pull-up: baseline
      - 10 strict pull-ups: solid intermediate
      - 20 strict pull-ups: strong
      - 30 strict pull-ups: excellent

      **Variations:**
      - **Chin-up:** Underhand grip, more bicep involvement.
      - **Weighted pull-up:** Adds load, builds max strength.
      - **Kipping pull-up:** Uses hip drive for higher reps (CrossFit style).
      - **L-sit pull-up:** Legs held straight out, adds core demand.
      - **Archer pull-up:** Unilateral loading, progresses toward one-arm.
      - **Muscle-up:** Transition from pull-up to dip.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Considerations"
    body: |
      **Muscle activation:**
      - Latissimus dorsi — primary mover
      - Biceps brachii — elbow flexion
      - Brachialis and brachioradialis — assist
      - Rhomboids, middle and lower trapezius — scapular retraction
      - Posterior deltoid — shoulder extension
      - Forearm flexors — grip

      **Scapular mechanics:**
      - Proper pull-up starts with scapular depression and retraction before elbow flexion.
      - "Packing" the shoulders protects the shoulder joint at the bottom of the movement.
      - Poor scapular control is a leading cause of shoulder pain in high-volume pull-up training.

      **Grip considerations:**
      - Bar thickness affects grip demand.
      - Fat grips increase forearm and grip demand.
      - Mixed grip not recommended for pull-ups (bicep tear risk).

      **Programming science:**
      - Pull-up volume can be high without excessive systemic fatigue.
      - Frequency of 2–3× per week is well-tolerated.
      - Negatives and weighted variations drive strength adaptation most efficiently.
      - Kipping pull-ups trade strict strength for work capacity — good for metabolic conditioning, poor for max strength.

      **Physiological adaptation:**
      - Grip strength and connective tissue in the forearms adapt slower than muscle.
      - Elbow tendinopathy is common in high-volume pull-up training; manage volume and add eccentric work.
