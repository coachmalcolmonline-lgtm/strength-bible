    title: "What Is the Bodyweight Squat?"
    body: |
      The bodyweight squat — also called the air squat — is exactly what it sounds like. You squat down and stand up. No bar. No weight. Just you.

      This is where every athlete starts. Before you ever put a barbell on your back, you have to own the bodyweight squat.

      **Why it matters:**
      - Teaches the fundamental squat pattern
      - Builds leg strength and mobility
      - Prepares you for the back squat, front squat, and Olympic lifts
      - Can be done anywhere, anytime

      The bodyweight squat is not a beginner exercise you graduate from. Elite athletes still do air squats — in warm-ups, as conditioning, and as technique drills.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Bodyweight Squat"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Stance: shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out
      - Chest up, back arched
      - Arms out in front for balance (or crossed over chest)

      **Execution:**
      - Take a breath, brace
      - Send butt back and down
      - Knees track over feet
      - Descend until hip crease is below knee
      - Drive through heels, stand up
      - Full extension at top

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Lumbar curve maintained
      - Weight in heels
      - Depth below parallel
      - Knees track over feet

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Losing lumbar curve | Lift chest, engage hip flexors, raise arms as you descend |
      | Weight shifts to toes | Exaggerate heels, float toes slightly |
      | Not low enough | Cue "Lower!"; squat to a 10" box |
      | Knees cave inward | Cue "Push knees out"; activate glute med with banded work; cue "Screw feet into the ground" |

      **The Tabata Standard:**
      The classic measure of bodyweight squat mastery: Tabata Squats. 20 seconds of work, 10 seconds rest, 8 rounds. The weakest interval should be 18–20 perfect squats. That's 18–20 squats in 20 seconds with perfect form — no cheating.

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming & Variations"
    body: |
      **Training role:**
      - Warm-up: activate legs and hips before barbell work.
      - Conditioning: high-rep bodyweight squats are brutal metabolic work.
      - Technique: reinforces the squat pattern without load.
      - Deload: maintains pattern while reducing stress.

      **Programming:**
      - 3–5 sets of 15–25 reps for warm-up or conditioning.
      - Tabata squats for metabolic conditioning.
      - Slow tempo (4-second descent) for mobility and control.
      - Paused squats (hold at bottom) for strength in the hole.

      **Variations:**
      - **Jump squat:** Explosive, develops power.
      - **Pistol squat:** One-legged, requires balance and strength.
      - **Cossack squat:** Side-to-side, hits adductors.
      - **Deep squat (hold):** Mobility-focused.
      - **Goblet squat:** Adds load in front, teaches upright posture.

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Considerations"
    body: |
      **Muscle activation:**
      - Quadriceps (vastus lateralis, medialis, intermedius, rectus femoris) — primary movers
      - Gluteus maximus — hip extension
      - Hamstrings — hip extension and knee stabilization
      - Adductors — knee and hip stability
      - Erector spinae — spinal stabilization
      - Calves — ankle stability

      **Motor control:**
      - The bodyweight squat is a closed-chain movement that requires coordinated hip, knee, and ankle mobility.
      - Ankle dorsiflexion restriction is a common limiter — if the ankle can't flex, the heel lifts or the torso compensates.
      - Hip flexion restriction (tight hip flexors) can cause the pelvis to tuck under (butt wink).
      - Thoracic extension matters for maintaining upright posture.

      **Programming science:**
      - Bodyweight squat volume can be very high without significant recovery cost.
      - Ideal for beginners, elderly populations, and as a foundational movement in any program.
      - Tabata protocol (20:10 × 8) develops both aerobic and anaerobic capacity.
      - Jump squats and other plyometric variations develop rate of force development and lower-body power.

      **Longevity:**
      - Deep squat ability correlates strongly with functional independence in older adults.
      - The squat pattern preserves hip, knee, and ankle health across the lifespan.
      - Regular squatting (loaded or unloaded) is one of the best protectors against age-related mobility loss.
