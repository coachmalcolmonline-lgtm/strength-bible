    title: "What Is the Hanging Knee Tuck?"
    body: |
      The hanging knee tuck is a core exercise done from a pull-up bar. You hang, then pull your knees up toward your chest.

      Simple? The movement is. Holding the hang isn't. Most athletes can't hang for 30 seconds on their first attempt.

      **Why it matters:**

      - Trains the abs harder than crunches or sit-ups
      - Trains grip strength (you're hanging)
      - Teaches core-to-limb connection (abs engage while the arms stay relaxed)
      - Builds toward toes-to-bar, an elite core movement
      - No equipment except a bar

      **The difference from crunches:** the hanging knee tuck forces your core to work against gravity while your entire lower body hangs. Your abs have to curl your pelvis. That's real core work.

      **Scaling note:** Can't hang yet? Start with an assisted hang (feet on box) or a dead hang hold to build grip. Can't get your knees up? Start with partial knee raises. The scaled version is the rung -- not the ceiling.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Hanging Knee Tuck"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Hang from a pull-up bar with an overhand grip, hands shoulder-width apart
      - Full dead hang at the start
      - Shoulders engaged (not just hanging limp)
      - Core tight

      **Execution:**
      1. From the hang, pull your knees up toward your chest
      2. Curl the pelvis up and back (don't just lift the legs)
      3. Hold the top position for 1 second
      4. Lower under control back to a full hang
      5. Reset (no swinging)

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Full dead hang at the bottom of each rep
      - Knee tuck is controlled -- no swinging or kipping
      - Pelvis curls (lower abs engage)
      - Shoulders stay active
      - Reset between reps

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Swinging | Cue "Slow down, control the descent" |
      | Only lifting the legs (no pelvic curl) | Cue "Curl your hips up toward your ribs" |
      | Half range of motion | Cue "Knees to chest" |
      | Hanging limp | Cue "Squeeze shoulders down and back" |
      | Gripping too hard | Cue "Relaxed grip, tight core" |

      **Progressions:**
      - Dead hang hold (build to 30-60 sec)
      - Bent-knee tucks (knees up, partial)
      - Full hanging knee tuck (knees to chest)
      - Hanging straight-leg raise (toes to bar height)
      - Toes-to-bar
      - Hanging wipers

      **Reps:** 3x8-12 for volume. 5x3-5 for strength. Always full range.

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming & Function"
    body: |
      **Training role:**
      - Core strength: abs, hip flexors, obliques
      - Grip endurance: dead hang under tension
      - Skill work: builds toward toes-to-bar and advanced core movements
      - Shoulder stability: active hang position

      **Programming:**
      - Volume: 3x10-15 or 5x5-8
      - Strength focus: 5x3-5 with a pause at the top
      - Warm-up: 2x5 easy tucks before advanced core work
      - Combined with pull-ups: 3 rounds of pull-ups + tucks

      **What it trains:**
      - Rectus abdominis (pelvic curl)
      - Hip flexors (raising the legs)
      - Obliques (rotational stabilization)
      - Forearm flexors (grip)
      - Lats (active hang, shoulder depression)
      - Shoulders (stability)

      **Why it matters for athletes:**
      - Core strength transfers to every sport
      - Hanging core work builds the midline that protects the spine
      - Grip endurance carries over to carries, deadlifts, and pull-ups
      - Toes-to-bar is a high-level gymnastics movement that requires this base

      **Benchmarks:**
      - 30 sec dead hang: baseline
      - 10 unbroken hanging knee tucks: intermediate
      - 15 unbroken with full pelvic curl: strong
      - 10 toes-to-bar: advanced

      **Scaling for beginners:**
      - If you can't hang: build grip strength with dead hangs (5x20-30 sec)
      - If you can hang but can't tuck: partial knee raises
      - If full tucks are easy: straighten the legs (toes-to-bar)

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Considerations"
    body: |
      **Muscle activation:**
      - Rectus abdominis: primary for pelvic curl
      - Iliopsoas and rectus femoris: hip flexors raise the legs
      - External and internal obliques: rotational and lateral stability
      - Latissimus dorsi: active hang, shoulder depression
      - Forearm flexors: grip endurance
      - Serratus anterior: scapular control

      **EMG research:**
      - Hanging leg raises produce higher rectus abdominis activation than crunches, sit-ups, or planks.
      - The pelvic curl component is critical -- just lifting the legs uses more hip flexor than ab.
      - The unstable hang position increases stabilizer demand significantly.

      **The hip flexor vs. ab question:**

      A common mistake is to think hanging leg raises only train abs. They also train hip flexors heavily. This is fine -- hip flexors are important for sprinting and kicking -- but athletes with tight hip flexors should balance this work with hip extension work (bridges, RDLs).

      **Variations for specific adaptations:**
      - **Bent-knee tuck:** starting point for beginners
      - **Straight-leg raise:** more hip flexor, more range of motion
      - **Toes-to-bar:** elite gymnastics, requires hip flexor strength + ab control + grip
      - **Hanging windshield wipers:** adds rotation, obliques
      - **Weighted hanging tucks:** add ankle weights for progressive overload
      - **Hanging L-sit:** static hold with legs extended at 90 degrees

      **Programming science:**
      - Grip is the limiting factor for most athletes -- build dead hangs in parallel.
      - Hanging core work should be 2-3x per week max.
      - Volume tolerance is lower than ground-based core work due to grip fatigue.
      - Pair with pull-ups in the same session for time efficiency.
      - Deload hanging work every 4-6 weeks -- forearms and elbows need recovery.

      **Injury considerations:**
      - Elbow tendonitis is common with high-volume hanging work. Stop if elbows ache.
      - Shoulder impingement can occur if the active hang position isn't trained. Warm up thoroughly.
      - Grip failure is a safety issue -- always use a bar with a mat underneath.
