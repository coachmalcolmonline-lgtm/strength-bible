    body: |
      The push-up is the oldest upper-body exercise in the world. You start in a plank position, lower your chest to the ground, and press back up.

      It builds chest, shoulders, triceps, and core. And it requires zero equipment.

      **Why it matters:**
      - Builds upper body pushing strength
      - Teaches full-body tension (core stays tight the whole time)
      - Progresses from the knees to the toes to feet elevated to one-arm
      - Transfers to the bench press, the press, and the dip

      Push-ups are not a beginner exercise. Done correctly, they're brutally hard.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Push-up"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Hands slightly wider than shoulder-width
      - Body in a straight line from head to heels
      - Core tight, glutes squeezed

      **Execution:**
      - Lower chest to the ground (elbows track back at ~45 degrees)
      - Touch the ground if possible
      - Press back up to full lockout
      - Body stays straight the entire time

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Body straight (no sagging hips, no raised butt)
      - Full range of motion (chest to ground)
      - Elbows track back, not flared out
      - Full lockout at top

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Sagging hips | Cue "Tighten core, squeeze glutes" |
      | Partial range of motion | Cue "Chest to ground" |
      | Elbows flaring out | Cue "Elbows back at 45 degrees" |
      | Head dropping | Cue "Neutral neck" |

      **Progressions:**
      - Knee push-up → standard push-up → feet elevated → deficit push-up → one-arm push-up
      - If standard push-ups are too hard: incline push-ups (hands on a bench)
      - If standard push-ups are too easy: elevate feet, add pauses, or slow the eccentric

  - level: 3
    title: "Programming & Scaling"
    body: |
      **Training role:**
      - Push-up is the bodyweight equivalent of the bench press.
      - Excellent for upper body hypertrophy, muscular endurance, and warm-ups.
      - Trains core stability under dynamic load.

      **Programming:**
      - 3–5 sets of 8–20 reps is standard.
      - For strength: add load (weighted vest, chains) or progress to harder variations.
      - For endurance: higher reps, shorter rest.
      - For skill: harder variations (archer, pseudo-planche, one-arm).

      **Benchmarks:**
      - 50 push-ups unbroken: solid intermediate standard
      - 75 push-ups: strong
      - 100 push-ups: excellent

      **Common scaling for beginners:** Knee push-ups, incline push-ups, or negative-only push-ups (start at top, lower slowly, reset).

  - level: 4
    title: "Advanced Considerations"
    body: |
      **Muscle activation:**
      - Pectoralis major (sternal and clavicular heads), anterior deltoid, triceps brachii are primary movers.
      - Serratus anterior is critical for scapular stability and protraction at the top of the movement.
      - Rotator cuff stabilizes the humeral head throughout.

      **EMG research:**
      - Push-ups produce comparable pectoral activation to the bench press at similar relative loads.
      - Stability demand is higher in push-ups than bench press due to the unstable base.
      - Core activation during push-ups is significantly higher than during bench press.

      **Variations for specific adaptations:**
      - **Deficit push-ups:** Greater range of motion, more stretch on pecs.
      - **Explosive push-ups (clap, superman):** Develop rate of force development and power.
      - **Archer push-ups:** Unilateral loading, progresses toward one-arm push-up.
      - **Pseudo-planche push-ups:** Forward shoulder lean increases anterior deltoid and core demand.
      - **Ring push-ups:** Instability increases stabilization demand.

      **Programming science:** Push-up volume can be very high without joint stress (unlike heavy bench press). Excellent for accumulating upper body volume without the recovery cost of heavy barbell work.
