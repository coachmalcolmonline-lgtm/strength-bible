    title: "What Is the Deep Squat?"
    body: |
      The deep squat is the bottom position of a squat, held for time. No load. No movement. Just sit at the bottom and stay there.

      This is not the "third-world squat." Call it the deep squat. It's a natural human position — how most of the world still sits to eat, rest, and socialize.

      The deep squat is:
      - A mobility drill
      - A stretch for the hips, ankles, and adductors
      - A diagnostic tool — what's tight shows up immediately
      - A position you can improve with practice

      Most Western adults can't hold a deep squat for 30 seconds. That's a problem. It means the hips, ankles, or adductors have lost range.

  - level: 2
    title: "How to Perform the Deep Squat"
    body: |
      **Setup:**
      - Feet shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out
      - Heels on the ground
      - Chest up, back arched

      **Execution:**
      - Squat down to the bottom
      - Sit there
      - Hold for time — start with 30 seconds, build to 2–5 minutes
      - Breathe

      **Primary Points of Performance:**
      - Heels stay down (if not, ankle mobility issue)
      - Lumbar curve maintained (if not, hip flexion or core issue)
      - Knees track over feet
      - Relaxed upper body
      - Deep, controlled breathing

      **Common Faults & Fixes:**

      | Fault | Fix |
      |---|---|
      | Heels lift | Ankle mobility work — calf stretches, wall ankle dorsiflexion drills |
      | Butt wink (losing lumbar curve) | Reduce depth slightly, work on hip flexor and hamstring mobility |
      | Knees cave in | Activate glutes, cue "Push knees out" |
      | Unable to get low at all | Start with heels elevated on plates or a book |

      **Hold for time:** Start with 30 seconds. Progress to 60, then 90, then 2 minutes. The goal is comfortable, sustainable position — not torture.

  - level: 3
    title: "Why the Deep Squat Matters"
    body: |
      **Mobility demands:**
      - **Ankle dorsiflexion:** ~35–40 degrees for a full-depth squat.
      - **Hip flexion:** ~120+ degrees with adequate capsular mobility.
      - **Thoracic extension:** For upright torso.
      - **Adductor flexibility:** For depth in a moderate-width stance.

      **Why it's a diagnostic:**
      - The deep squat position exposes every mobility restriction in the lower body and trunk.
      - A tight ankle shows up as a lifted heel.
      - A tight hip shows up as a butt wink or forward lean.
      - A tight thoracic spine shows up as a rounded upper back.
      - A tight adductor shows up as a wide stance or inability to get depth.

      **Programming role:**
      - Warm-up: opens hips, ankles, and thoracic spine before training.
      - Cool-down: relaxed stretch post-training.
      - Assessment: shows changes in mobility over time.
      - Restorative: undoes some of the damage of prolonged sitting.

      **Frequency:** Daily. The deep squat is a practice, not a once-a-week exercise.

  - level: 4
    title: "Research & Longevity"
    body: |
      **Research context:**
      - The deep squat position is a fundamental human resting posture, found in nearly all pre-industrial societies.
      - Studies show that populations who regularly squat to depth have lower rates of hip and knee osteoarthritis, not higher.
      - The deep squat is now used as a functional assessment in physical therapy and movement screening.

      **Biomechanical rationale:**
      - At full depth, the hip capsule, glute max, and hamstrings are loaded in a stretch position, promoting connective tissue adaptation.
      - The ankle in full dorsiflexion loads the talocrural joint and Achilles tendon, improving their capacity.
      - The adductors, at depth, are at end-range and benefit from static loading.

      **Longevity implications:**
      - Loss of squat depth correlates strongly with loss of functional independence.
      - Deep squat practice preserves hip mobility, ankle function, and lower body coordination across the lifespan.
      - Many traditional cultures use the deep squat as a daily position (eating, cooking, socializing) — and show dramatically better mobility in old age than Western populations.

      **Programming notes:**
      - Daily practice is best. Short sessions frequently > long sessions occasionally.
      - The position is self-limiting — if you can't get there, you can't get there. Don't force depth; work on the limiting factor.
      - Combine with SMR (adductors, hip flexors, calves) for best results.
